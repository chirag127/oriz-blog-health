You do not need a gym, a coach, or a complicated program to start getting
stronger. You need five basic movements, two sessions a week, and a way to make
each week slightly harder than the last. That last part — progressive overload
— is the whole game. Everything else is detail.

## The five patterns

A good beginner routine covers the movements your body actually uses, not a
long list of isolated exercises:

- **Squat** — bodyweight squat, then goblet squat holding a weight.
- **Hinge** — Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, or single-leg deadlift.
- **Push** — push-up (against a wall or bench first if needed), then floor or
  overhead press.
- **Pull** — inverted row under a sturdy table, band row, or backpack row.
- **Carry / core** — a loaded carry, plank, or dead bug.

Pick one from each pattern. That is your whole workout. Home-friendly loads —
a backpack full of books, a couple of water jugs, resistance bands, or a single
adjustable dumbbell — are enough to start and to progress for months.

## Sets, reps, and effort

For a beginner, the exact numbers matter less than showing up and adding a
little each week. A reasonable starting point:

- **Frequency:** full body, twice a week, with at least a day between sessions.
- **Sets:** 2–3 working sets per movement.
- **Reps:** 6–10 for most exercises — enough to practise good form without
  grinding.
- **Effort:** stop each set with about 1–3 reps left in the tank. You should
  not be training to failure while you are still learning the movement.
- **Rest:** 1–2 minutes between hard sets.

Twice a week is not a compromise — for a beginner it is genuinely effective,
because it spreads practice and recovery well. Training each pattern about twice
a week lines up with what public-health and training guidance recommends.

## Progressive overload, the simple version

Here is the only rule you need for progress: **when a workout starts to feel
easy, make the next one slightly harder.**

Concretely — when you can complete all your sets and reps with clean form, do
one of these next session:

1. Add a rep (until you hit the top of your range), **or**
2. Add a small amount of load — a heavier book in the bag, a thicker band, a
   step up in dumbbell weight, **or**
3. Make the movement harder — feet elevated on push-ups, a slower lowering
   phase, a single-leg version.

A common linear progression is roughly a small bump to upper-body lifts and a
slightly larger one to lower-body lifts once you complete the work cleanly. Keep
a note — phone, notebook, anything. What you did last time is the whole plan for
this time.

## A sample week

| Day | Movements |
|-----|-----------|
| Session A | Goblet squat · Push-up · Backpack row · Plank |
| Rest | Walk, stretch, or nothing |
| Session B | Romanian deadlift · Overhead press · Inverted row · Loaded carry |

Same five patterns, small variations. Run it for eight weeks before you change
anything.

## What to skip early on

- **Training to failure every set.** It buys little for beginners and costs a
  lot in recovery and form.
- **Long, fancy split routines.** Full-body twice a week beats a six-day
  bodybuilder split you cannot recover from or stick to.
- **Worrying about the "optimal" rep range.** For strength, heavier and lower
  reps; for size, moderate reps. As a beginner you improve across all of them.
  Consistency beats optimisation.

Start light, keep good form, add a little each week. If something hurts sharply
(not just muscle fatigue), stop and let it settle. If you have a heart
condition, a recent injury, or you are new to exercise after a long gap, check
with a doctor before you start. This is general information, not medical advice.
